My research applies finite element and multiphysics modelling to nonlinear static and dynamic problems. The central question is how thermal, hydraulic and mechanical processes drive permafrost degradation, and how this affects ground stability and infrastructure performance in cold regions.

I use OpenGeoSys to build and calibrate THM models against field data, including a field-scale study of pore-water salinity effects on active-layer and permafrost dynamics at Norman Wells, Northwest Territories. A parallel line of work examines geothermal energy for low-carbon heating and cooling in cold-region and urban microclimates.

A

Computational geomechanics

In plain terms: computers are used to predict how the ground moves, fails and carries water when it is loaded or heated.

Computational geomechanics uses numerical methods to predict how soil and rock deform, fail and transmit fluid under load, groundwater change and temperature change. Closed-form solutions rarely exist for layered, nonlinear, coupled ground, so the problem is discretised and solved on a computer.

A reliable analysis is a chain of decisions, from the conceptual model to the constitutive laws and the numerical scheme. Each link needs checking, and the results are only as defensible as the calibration and validation behind them.

Verification, validation and judgement

Verification asks whether the equations are solved correctly, for example by comparing with analytical or benchmark solutions. Validation asks whether the right equations and parameters are used, by comparing with field or laboratory data. Software and AI tools can speed up scripting and analysis, but checking the result and judging its limits remain the engineer’s responsibility.

B

Thermo-hydro-mechanical modelling

In plain terms: heat, water and stress in the ground affect one another, so they are solved together instead of one at a time.

Thermo-hydro-mechanical (THM) modelling solves heat transfer, fluid flow and deformation together. In a porous geomaterial the three fields influence each other: temperature changes fluid properties and, near 0 °C, the ice–water phase; flowing water transports heat; and pore-water pressure changes effective stress, which controls deformation and strength.

Deformation feeds back through porosity and permeability, and the material properties themselves depend on temperature, saturation and ice content. Treating the fields separately can miss exactly the behaviour that matters, such as thaw settlement and pore-pressure build-up.

Governing balances

A THM formulation is built from three balance laws: conservation of energy for heat, conservation of mass for the pore fluid, and momentum balance (equilibrium) for the soil skeleton. Constitutive relations close the system by relating stress to strain, heat flux to temperature gradient, and fluid flux to pressure gradient.

C

Permafrost and frozen soils

In plain terms: ground that stays frozen year-round is thawing in a warming climate, and that changes how safe buildings, roads and pipelines are on it.

Permafrost is ground that stays at or below 0 °C for at least two consecutive years. Above it lies the active layer, which thaws each summer and refreezes in winter. The permafrost table marks the boundary between them, and the thaw front is the advancing limit of thawing during the warm season.

Ice content governs strength, stiffness and thermal behaviour. When ice-rich ground thaws, excess pore water can leave the soil weaker and more compressible, which affects foundations, embankments and pipelines. Dissolved salts lower the freezing point of pore water, so a saline soil can hold unfrozen water below 0 °C. My conference paper examines pore-water salinity effects at Norman Wells, Northwest Territories, using a THM model.

D

Numerical modelling and FEM

In plain terms: the ground is split into many small pieces so a computer can solve equations that cannot be solved by hand.

The finite element method (FEM) divides a domain into elements, approximates the unknown fields with shape functions, and assembles equations that balance forces, fluxes or heat. Constitutive models enter at the integration points of each element.

Good results depend on choices that can be inspected: mesh refinement where gradients are steep, boundary conditions placed far enough away not to control the answer, time steps small enough for transient processes, and iteration tolerances for nonlinear behaviour. A mesh and boundary study should accompany any serious simulation.

E

Geothermal energy

In plain terms: the ground below a few metres stays at a steady temperature, so it can supply heating in winter and cooling in summer.

Below the depth of seasonal temperature change, ground temperature is nearly steady. Ground-source and geothermal systems use this by exchanging heat with the ground through boreholes or other heat exchangers, to supply heating in winter and cooling in summer.

Performance depends on ground thermal conductivity, heat capacity, groundwater movement and the temperatures at which the system operates. In cold regions, freezing around heat exchangers and the thermal response of the surrounding ground also need to be considered. My Loyola Campus work contributed field investigation, with exploratory drilling and sample collection, to a geothermal feasibility assessment.

F

Soil–structure interaction

In plain terms: a building and the ground under it push on each other, and both move as a result.

Soil–structure interaction describes how a structure and the ground respond to one another. The foundation transmits load to the soil, the soil settles and redistributes stress, and that movement in turn changes the forces in the structure.

Key quantities are the contact stress at the interface, the stress distribution with depth, settlement and differential settlement, and the stiffness of the soil relative to the foundation. Interface behaviour, including slip or separation, can matter as much as soil stiffness.

Terms

Short glossary

Active layer
Near-surface ground that thaws in summer and refreezes in winter.
Permafrost
Ground at or below 0 °C for at least two consecutive years.
Effective stress
Total stress minus pore-water pressure; it controls soil deformation and strength.
Pore-water pressure
Pressure of the water held in the voids between soil grains.
Constitutive model
The mathematical rule relating stress to strain, and heat or fluid flux to their driving gradients.
Gauss point
A sampling point inside a finite element where stresses and material response are evaluated.
Thaw settlement
Ground surface lowering as ice melts and excess water drains.
Site response
How soil layers modify earthquake shaking on its way to the surface.
